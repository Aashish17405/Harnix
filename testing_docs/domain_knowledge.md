Virtual Accounts in Corporate Transaction Banking
Business and functional domain reference for the VCB Corporate Digital Banking programme
Purpose. This note explains how virtual accounts work as a transaction banking product before relating that knowledge to VCB’s signed-off virtual account Inquiry BRD. It is intended to give business analysts, engineers and Kiro the same banking vocabulary and understanding of the operating model. It is not a restatement of the BRD, a technical solution design or a proposed product backlog.
Basis of interpretation. The first part describes widely used transaction banking concepts and product variations, drawing on banking practice and publicly described offerings. The second part describes what the VCB BRD actually says, with specific references. 
Part I - Understanding virtual accounts as a Banking Product
1. Start with the problem the corporate is trying to solve
Think of a distributor supplying goods to several hundred retailers. The distributor sends invoices throughout the month and receives payments from customers through bank transfers. The money reaches its bank account, so from the bank’s perspective the collection may be complete. But the Accounts Receivable (AR) team has a different problem: it needs to determine which customer paid, which invoice or invoices the payment relates to, and whether anything remains outstanding. A bank statement may contain an abbreviated payer name, an unhelpful narration, or a single transfer covering several invoices. Identifying the receipt can take longer than receiving it.
There are traditional ways of addressing this which is asking customers to quote the invoice number, assigning payment references, or maintaining multiple bank accounts for different collection activities. Each method has limitations. References are often mistyped or lost on the payment journey, and opening separate current accounts for every retailer, project or outlet quickly becomes impractical. More accounts mean more administration, signatories, statements, bank charges and reconciliation effort. They can also leave the treasury function with a fragmented picture of cash.
virtual accounts address the identification and reporting problem without necessarily creating another deposit account each time the business wants to track a separate stream of collections. The bank associates a unique virtual account identifier with the corporate’s underlying current or settlement account. The payer can use that identifier when making an eligible payment. When the credit arrives, the bank can attribute it to the virtual account and therefore to the customer, project, outlet or other purpose to which the corporate has assigned it. Actual funds are booked to the underlying account in a typical collections arrangement; the virtual identity allows the transaction to be reported separately.
This is why virtual accounts are often described as a collections and reconciliation product, even though more sophisticated versions also help corporates manage cash across divisions, subsidiaries and in-house banking structures. Their first value is not that they move money faster. It is that they give a large number of transactions a meaningful business identity, so a corporate can recognize and act on a receipt with less manual investigation.
A simple example illustrates the difference. A wholesaler receives three payments of VND 40 million, VND 30 million and VND 15 million from three customers. Without reliable identifiers, finance sees VND 85 million in incoming cash but may need to investigate who paid. If each customer has a separate virtual account number, those three credits can be reported against the corresponding customer identifiers while the same VND 85 million is reflected in the linked current account. This does not automatically settle the customers’ outstanding invoices in the ERP. The next step is to match the credited amounts with the business receivables.
2. What is a virtual account (VA)?
A virtual account is a logical banking identifier or virtual sub-account maintained in association with a real bank account. In the common receivables model, it helps distinguish incoming payments and support account-level or customer-level reporting. In a broader virtual account management (VAM) model, the bank may also maintain a subledger showing attributed balances and movements for departments, entities or business purposes. The underlying physical account remains the place where the bank holds the legal deposit and carries out settlement, unless a different product and legal arrangement has been expressly established.
This distinction matters because the word account can mislead even experienced project teams. A physical current account has a bank ledger balance and may support debit transactions, account mandates, credit limits and applicable banking terms. A virtual account may display what looks like a balance, but that value may be a reporting or subledger position. It should not be treated as a second pool of spendable money. A virtual reporting hierarchy is also not, by itself, an escrow structure, a trust arrangement or legally safeguarded client money.
Nor is a virtual account the same thing as an invoice number. An invoice is a commercial claim in the corporate’s ERP. A virtual account is a banking identifier that can help associate a payment with a payer or collection purpose. One payer may settle five invoices in one transfer; another may pay only part of one invoice; a third may send a payment for which the amount or reference does not match the ERP. The virtual account can help establish who paid without necessarily establishing which receivable is fully cleared. That distinction is central to the design of reconciliation processes.
Banks do not all offer the same model. A collections-oriented virtual account product may focus almost entirely on inbound payments and reporting. A hierarchical VAM product may assign virtual subledger positions to individual business units or entities and support more elaborate account structures. Some advanced offerings also support outgoing payment attribution, on-behalf-of structures and treasury liquidity use cases. Those capabilities depend on the bank, jurisdiction, product configuration and underlying payment arrangements. Their existence in the industry must not be used to infer that any given VCB inquiry requirement supports debit initiation or virtual account creation.


3. Where the product sits within Transaction Banking
For the corporate, virtual accounts sit at the intersection of Cash Management, Collections, Receivables, Account Services and Treasury. Each function looks at the same transaction from a slightly different angle.
The collections team wants a dependable way to receive and identify funds. AR wants to apply the receipt against customer balances. The Treasury department wants to know how much cash is available in the physical account and which business activities generated it. The business unit manager wants to see collections for a particular branch, property or market without gaining visibility into every other part of the group. The CFO and controller want auditable figures and reliable supporting documents. The bank provides the underlying account, payment processing, reporting data and access controls that allow those questions to be answered appropriately.
Consider a property company with several developments. It may assign a virtual account to a buyer or a property unit, while all eligible collections settle to a designated operating account. The property collections team can review who has paid a scheduled installment. Treasury sees the bank cash position and consolidated inflows. Finance still has to determine whether that payment covers the correct installment, whether penalties or fees apply, and whether the buyer has overpaid. The same identifier helps several teams, but it does not replace their different business rules.
Another use case is a large corporate group using hierarchical VAM reporting. A group might want to view collections by subsidiary, country, business line and customer, even where funds are concentrated in a smaller number of physical bank accounts. The reporting hierarchy can mirror those management dimensions. However, legal-entity ownership and whether funds may be combined are separate questions. Cross-entity pooling, intercompany receivables and legal ring-fencing require explicit banking, treasury and regulatory decisions. Merely displaying two entities together on a dashboard does not mean their money may be moved or legally netted.
This distinction between cash movement, transaction attribution and corporate accounting is the most useful mental model for the product. They are connected activities, but they are not interchangeable and may be supported by different systems.
4. How the account structure is typically organized
Every implementation needs a relationship between the virtual account identifier and the actual account that will receive the funds. The simplest design is many virtual account identifiers mapped to one physical current account. A more developed structure introduces a root identifier or a parent-and-child hierarchy so the corporate can report at different levels. The hierarchy may correspond to regions, stores, subsidiaries, contracts or customers; it need not be a literal banking account for each node.
For example, a distributor could have one VND settlement account with two regional reporting parents. Under the North parent it tracks Retailers A and B; under the South parent it tracks Retailer C. The treasury user can look at the total credited to the settlement account, while the collections manager can examine the transactions attributed to a specific retailer. A parent view may aggregate child information, but whether aggregation includes only entitled descendants, how duplicates are prevented and how missing child data is handled are product rules, not universal banking truths.
The identifier format is likewise bank-specific. Some institutions use a bank or product prefix combined with a corporate-specific code and an end-customer reference. Others issue numbers according to their own length, character or payment-network restrictions. There may be limits on alphanumeric values, effective dates, uniqueness or reuse after closure. A payment rail may not preserve every character supported by an internal virtual account master. Consequently the number displayed in a portal, the beneficiary identifier used in the payment, and the account mapping stored by the bank must be designed consistently.
A further complication is currency. Some virtual accounts simply inherit the currency of the linked settlement account. Other products can present different currency views or support more complex arrangements. A VA identifier never by itself proves that automatic foreign exchange conversion is provided. If a bank receives a currency that is unsupported for the relevant account and payment rail, the outcome comes from the applicable payment and account rules.
5. Follow one incoming payment from the payer to the corporate ledger
The journey begins before the money moves. The corporate has an underlying banking relationship and an eligible current account. The corporate or the bank establishes the virtual account arrangement and assigns identifiers to payers, contracts or organisational units. That identifier must then be communicated to the person making the payment, typically through an invoice, payment instruction, customer portal or other established collection channel. The bank’s master data needs to know which current account and reporting identity correspond to the number.
When the payer submits the transfer, the payment travels through the relevant bank or clearing rail. The receiving bank validates the information it receives, handles the payment according to its product and scheme rules, and attempts to resolve the beneficiary or virtual account mapping. In a standard collections arrangement, accepted funds are credited to the underlying physical account. Separately, the bank’s reporting or virtual account layer associates the transaction with the appropriate virtual identifier and hierarchy. The corporate can then see the transaction in its banking channel or receive it through files or APIs, depending on the services subscribed to.
It is tempting to describe this as one uninterrupted, real-time step. Operationally it may be several events: the payment was received, the physical account was credited, the VA attribution was completed, the transaction became visible in the channel, and an alert was delivered. Those events can occur at different times. The bank may have successfully booked the money while a reporting feed is delayed. Conversely, a channel may show an in-progress or recently received transaction that is not yet finally settled, depending on the bank's defined data states. The design needs an authoritative understanding of which event underlies every status and metric.
Once the bank supplies the collection information, the corporate reconciliation process starts. The AR system may identify the customer from the VA number and try to match the credit to outstanding invoices using amount, currency, payment reference and due date. A full match may be applied automatically; a short payment, combined payment or uncertain allocation may go into an exceptions queue. The bank's contribution is accurate banking and attribution data. The corporate's responsibility is normally the commercial application of that receipt to its own receivables, unless additional reconciliation services are explicitly part of the product.
Reversals and corrections complicate this journey. If a credited payment is subsequently reversed, the initial collection view and the AR posting must not be treated as permanently final without a defined correction process. The bank reporting layer needs to preserve references and dates sufficiently for the corporate to understand the relationship between the original transaction and the correcting entry. How a reversal changes logical balances, prior statements or notifications must be specified for the particular offering.
6. What the different personas need from the service
The primary user of virtual account inquiry is not always the person who originally requested the account structure. In a large corporate, treasury may own the bank relationship, while the receivables team uses the product every day. Their questions differ. Treasury cares about group-wide inflows, concentration and cash position; AR cares about identifying the payer; the reconciliation analyst needs transaction references, value dates, amount and counterparty information; and the financial controller needs reliable evidence for period-end close and audit.
A corporate channel administrator has another responsibility: ensuring that each staff member sees only the correct company, account or part of the hierarchy. A regional collections officer may need visibility over one region's VAs but not the accounts of other subsidiaries. Corporate IT or the ERP integration team is concerned with extract formats, stable identifiers, data refreshes and what happens when a bank file arrives twice or an interface fails. A business-unit user may only need a simple view of collections under their responsibility.
The external payer is also a persona in the wider domain, but generally does not log into the corporate's Digital Banking portal. The payer needs correct beneficiary details, a usable number and assurance that its payment can be traced. If the payer uses the wrong or an old number, the issue enters bank operations and corporate investigation channels.
On the bank side, the cash management product team defines which virtual account models and controls are offered. Client onboarding and account operations establish the approved customer-account arrangements. Payment operations investigate unmatched or problematic credits. Client servicing and the relationship team handle corporate inquiries. Security, compliance and audit functions govern who can access bank and counterparty data, and how access, downloads, changes and incidents are evidenced.
These are business personas, not automatic system permissions. A label such as Maker, Checker, Approver or Viewer does not, by itself, tell us whether that user may see all VAs, download a statement or access another current account. Entitlements have to be explicitly modeled against the customer and account hierarchy; approval controls are relevant only where an actual approval activity is in scope.


7. What users do after the money has been received
Most business users experience virtual accounts through inquiry, not through the payment-processing engine. They enter a bank channel, select an entity or account that they are entitled to access, and move from a broad collection picture to more detailed records. A reasonable progression is an overview of relevant accounts, a list of associated virtual identities, an individual account view, and then its transactions, statements or account documents. That progression is conceptually common; the exact screens and navigation are channel-specific.
At summary level, a treasurer may ask how much money has come in today and whether it came from one part of the group or many. A list view is operationally useful when there are hundreds or thousands of virtual identifiers. Search, filtering, sorting, saved layouts and favorite accounts are not merely cosmetic features: they shorten the time needed to locate the right payer or business unit. The channel must apply entitlement restrictions to search results as well as to the page currently being viewed. Searching across all pages should not expose information hidden by pagination or another entity's permissions.
A detail view explains what a selected virtual account represents, which current account it links to, and what recent activity is attributed to it. Transaction history then provides evidence for investigation and reconciliation. A user may need to distinguish transaction date from value date, understand the transaction reference and determine whether a particular credit relates to one VA or a broader parent summary. If a transaction was posted late or backdated, the presentation must follow the bank's defined accounting and reporting rules rather than a convenient UI assumption.
Statements, exports and confirmations support different tasks. A transaction history is an interactive or downloadable record of movements. A statement is a period-based bank document with defined presentation and calculation conventions. An account confirmation is evidence of account details or existence as of a date; it is not another name for transaction history. A digital signature may add assurance about the bank-issued document, but its eligibility, charge and verification method belong to a separate document-service policy.
8. Banking rules that determine correct behaviour
There is no single universal virtual account rulebook. The rules reflect the bank’s product model, the underlying settlement account, local payments infrastructure, corporate entitlements and legal constraints. For a project team, the objective is to recognize the families of rules that control behaviour and then establish which version the bank actually applies.
Account ownership and mapping. A VA must resolve the intended customer and physical account. The bank needs a policy for unique identifiers, valid formats, effective dates, allowed changes, expired or closed identifiers and historic mappings. Without a historical record, a late payment to a reassigned identifier could be attributed to the wrong customer. The permitted resolution of that situation might be rejection, suspense, repair or another controlled process. It is unsafe to select one without the bank's product rules.
Hierarchy and access. An entitlement to a parent does not necessarily imply unrestricted access to every descendant, and permissions may be based on a current account, root, parent or individual VA. A reporting figure aggregated from thousands of children must not leak the activity of children outside the user's permitted population. Multi-entity views raise additional questions about legal entity boundaries and consolidated amounts.
Payment status and transaction time. Banks can distinguish receipt time, posting date, host date, value date and digital-channel display time. Those are not interchangeable fields. Whether a transaction belongs in “today's collections,” a monthly total or a historical statement depends on an explicitly chosen basis, time zone and accounting calendar. Reversals, returned payments and backdated postings need defined treatment in those calculations.
Balances and cash. A physical ledger balance, an available balance and a virtual attributed balance answer different questions. A running balance on a VA statement could be a cumulative reporting figure from a zero opening position or a subledger position; neither gives the user authority to initiate a debit from the VA. Parent-level totals also need to avoid double counting when a transaction is displayed under both child and parent references.
Reporting and documents. The bank must define how far back transactions can be queried, how much history a single request can cover, how large exports can be, which formats are available and when a signed document is allowed. Large requests may be handled by background document generation, partitioning or other mechanisms, but these are solution choices that follow from agreed functional outcomes. The system also needs a position on unavailable historical data, no-result queries, incomplete extracts and failed document production.
Notifications and privacy. A credit alert should correspond to an agreed banking event and be delivered only to an entitled recipient. Duplicate events, delayed alerts and delivery failure must be distinguished from the underlying payment outcome. Search results, downloaded files and audit records may contain personal and commercial information, so access control cannot stop at the screen boundary.
9. Exceptions
An account identification problem illustrates why exceptions matter. Suppose a buyer copies an old virtual account number from an earlier invoice. The incoming payment may refer to an expired, closed or reassigned identifier. The bank has to decide whether the payment is rejected, accepted to a suspense position, routed to an underlying account for manual investigation, or handled through some other approved procedure. From the corporate's perspective, the money may be missing from the expected customer collection even if a bank has received it. What happens cannot be answered by the inquiry channel alone; it belongs to the end-to-end banking operating model.
Even after successful posting, an attribution problem may remain. The payment could appear under the physical account but not yet be associated with the correct VA because a reporting feed is delayed or a mapping is defective. That is not the same as a failed transfer. Similarly, an AR match failure is not necessarily a bank failure. A payer may use the correct VA but remit too little, too much, or one amount covering several invoices. The bank may provide sound transaction data while finance still has an unapplied-receipt exception.
For inquiry and servicing, common exceptions include users requesting accounts outside their entitlement, a selected VA no longer being accessible, empty transaction ranges, historical records beyond retention, downloads too large for normal synchronous response, document-signature service unavailability and duplicate or delayed notifications. Controls should ensure the user sees an accurate outcome: “no transactions,” “no access,” “data not available,” and “service temporarily unavailable” are materially different business situations.
A final exception arises when information is technically present but financially misleading. Imagine a statement with a fixed zero opening balance and a running balance calculated only within the chosen period. A user might interpret the closing number as the real deposit balance unless the reporting basis is clearly defined. The solution must not invent a financial meaning simply because a field is called “balance.” In transaction banking, incorrect interpretation can be more serious than a visible error message.
The practical discipline is to connect each exception to an owner and an evidence trail. Payment operations resolve booking and repair problems; the virtual account platform or reporting team resolves attribution and data issues; corporate AR resolves invoice allocation; channel support handles presentation or access failures. The precise division of responsibility varies by bank and must be documented rather than presumed.
10. Glossary of terms
Physical/current/settlement account. The real bank account in which the deposit is booked and from which supported banking transactions settle. The term settlement account emphasizes its purpose, while the current account identifies the relevant deposit product.
virtual account (VA). A unique logical identifier associated with an underlying bank account and used for transaction attribution, segmentation or subledger reporting. Its capabilities depend on the bank's product model.
virtual account Management (VAM). A broader cash-management arrangement that may maintain multiple virtual identifiers, hierarchy nodes and subledger positions against physical accounts; some implementations support advanced payables and liquidity reporting.
CIF (Customer Information File). The bank's record identifying a customer relationship. A corporate group can have more than one CIF depending on legal entity and bank structure; CIF is not synonymous with the staff user logging into a channel.
Root VA / Parent VA / Child VA. Levels of a virtual reporting and identification hierarchy. The root anchors a grouping, a parent groups subordinate identifiers, and a child is a more granular account reference. Exact generation rules, ownership and entitlement inheritance vary by bank.
Virtual balance / attributed balance / running balance. A reported accumulation or subledger value assigned to a virtual identity. It does not necessarily equal the physical account's ledger or available balance. The opening basis, debit/credit signs, hierarchy roll-up and reversal treatment determine its meaning.
Account receivable, cash application and reconciliation. A receivable is an amount owed by a buyer. Cash application allocates a receipt to that business obligation. Reconciliation compares banking receipts and ledger records to identify matched, outstanding and disputed items.
Remitter / payer / counterparty. The originator of an incoming payment. The payer's legal name, bank account name and the customer identifier used in the corporate ERP may not be identical.
Posting date / value date / host date / transaction timestamp. Different measures of when the bank processed, accounted for, valued or recorded a payment. They must not be used interchangeably in daily and monthly reporting.
Statement / transaction history / account confirmation. Respectively, a defined bank document for a period, a query or extract of transaction records, and a bank-issued confirmation of account information or status. They answer different business questions.
Digital signature. A mechanism used to authenticate a digital document under the applicable bank service and legal framework; having a PDF does not automatically mean it is digitally signed.
Maker / Checker / Approver / Viewer. Corporate channel roles commonly used for initiating, reviewing, authorizing and viewing activity. Their actual permissions depend on the bank's configured entitlement matrix and the functions in scope.
Part II - Applying the Domain to VCB’s virtual account Inquiry BRD
11. What VCB is trying to implement
VCB identifies existing corporate customers of its Corporate DigiBank platform as the users and lists Web and Mobile as applicable channels. The named corporate roles are Maker, Checker, Approver and Viewer. Corporate users must be onboarded, entitled to inquiry, and associated with a current account linked to a virtual account. The BRD also states that the VA service is a user interface aggregating data from the Corporate Banking System and working with configured entitlements. This establishes a clear direction: the channel must represent banking information accurately and protect access; it should not be treated as the authoritative book of physical cash by default. 
12. How VCB represents a virtual account
VCB provides a specific number composition in its objective section: virtual account Number = Bank Code (Root VA) + Customer Code, and Bank Code (Root VA) = VA Product Code + Customer Root VA. The Customer Root VA is associated with the CIF and may reflect a customer preference within bank-prescribed length constraints. The BRD does not provide the final character lengths, validation algorithm, reserved values or number-allocation procedure. The inquiry hierarchy runs from Current Account → Root VA → Parent VA → virtual account, and permissions can be controlled at Current Account, Root VA, Parent VA or individual VA level. A user may see different parts of the hierarchy based on configured entitlement. The corporate can operate under a single CIF or a group of CIFs; in a multi-CIF scenario the user should be able to view or select the entitled entities. The notion of an “entity” therefore needs to follow the operational context of a corporate legal entity/CIF.
The account-list data defines three virtual account types: Customer VA, Add on – Self VA, and Add on – On Behalf of VA. The BRD supplies these labels but not the functional rules that distinguish them, so their presence must not be interpreted as authorization to originate on-behalf-of payments. At detail level the BRD identifies the current account as the physical account where actual money resides and describes VA balances as logical reporting values. This is consistent with the broader operating distinction in Part I, though the exact calculation and presentation of VCB's virtual amounts require care. [VCB BRD §§5.2.1–5.2.3]
13. The VCB user’s inquiry journey
An entitled corporate user begins with the accounts and CIFs they can access. The BRD expects an aggregated summary and a view of the VA hierarchy with counts of virtual accounts linked to Parent VAs and Current Accounts, alongside the total count. In a group structure the user can select one or more authorized entities. Such a summary must be understood as a user-scoped picture, not necessarily a bank-wide or unrestricted corporate-group total. [VCB BRD §5.1 BF-AS-VA-01]
From there, the user moves to a consolidated list of linked virtual accounts. VCB sets a specific default ordering: favorite accounts first, then Active accounts ahead of other statuses, followed by opening date from newest to oldest. A closed virtual account remains visible for up to the last 12 months. The user can export the full list or selected accounts, and the document format matrix specifies PDF or XLSX for virtual account lists. These details matter because a user searching for a closed VA to investigate an older collection must still have a predictable way to find it. 
The list carries the key banking relationships: Current Account Number and Name, Bank Code/Root VA, Parent VA Number, VA Type, VA Name, VA Number, a user-defined nickname, settlement-account currency and status. The data table marks these fields mandatory for display, including Nickname, while nickname creation is optional in the servicing instructions. The sensible reading is that the field belongs to the view; the source does not establish what should be displayed when a nickname has never been defined. It would be inappropriate to turn that into a compulsory nickname-creation step. 
VCB expects partial keyword search across available information fields and across the account list, not merely the visible page. Users can filter by such values as CIF, Current Account, Root VA, VA Number, VA Name and nickname, and receive a notification when no records match. They can reorder and choose visible columns, have those preferences retained for later logins, reset them, and change rows per page between 10, 20 and 50, with 10 as the default. Favorite accounts and nicknames are stored at user-profile level. These behaviours are the everyday tools that allow a collections analyst to locate one relevant identifier from a long list. 
On opening an individual VA, the user sees its organizational and banking context: business registration identifier, corporate name, CIF, registered address, managing branch, VA and Current Account numbers, VA type, Root and Parent identifiers, account name and nickname, currency, opening date and status. VCB also asks for today's inflow, month-to-date credits and the last ten transactions, with a “View more” route to the full transaction history. Today's inflow and month-to-date credits are collection measures, not evidence that the VA holds an independently withdrawable deposit. Their date and time basis must match the data source and banking calendar. 
The BRD also permits a user to move from the VA view to its linked Current Account screen, but only when the user has the separate entitlement to do so. A user entitled to see a particular VA therefore must not be assumed to have full rights over the physical account to which it maps. The hierarchy is available in a separate tab, again subject to the configured access scope. 
14. Transaction inquiry, balances and evidence
When a corporate investigates a receipt, it needs more than the amount and date. VCB specifies transaction references, TellerID–Sequence, Branch–Host Date–PC Time, value date-time, counterparty account and name, correspondent bank, VA hierarchy values, debit and credit amounts, running balance, currency and narrative. The field definitions include display formatting: TellerID is 4 characters with leading zeroes, Sequence and branch are 5, Host Date is DD/MM/YYYY and PC Time is HH:MM:SS. These are presentation requirements for traceability, not permission to invent new transaction data. 
Transaction history supports default and preset period is seven days, fourteen days, last month and last three months alongside a custom range. A single inquiry is restricted to a maximum of twelve months. Elsewhere the BRD describes the historical availability as “3–5 years”; that is not a precise entitlement or retention rule until VCB chooses the applicable figure. Exports of transaction history are PDF and XLSX. Users can seek history at virtual account or Root VA/Parent level as stated in the document matrix, subject to the correct hierarchy and entitlement treatment. 
The most delicate question in this section is what the word balance means. VCB explicitly says that a VA does not hold its own funds, and its statement requirement says the opening balance is zero. The transaction-history field definitions also include closing balance and running balance; the running value is described as accumulated over the user-selected statement period. A zero opening value may be a convention for presenting attributed transactions in a chosen period, but the source does not fully explain whether debit entries, reversals or parent roll-ups are included in the same way, nor whether “closing balance” is intended to represent a period net amount. Kiro should learn the distinction, preserve the stated zero opening requirement, and avoid inventing a physical deposit balance for the VA. [VCB BRD §§3.2.2, 5.1 BF-AS-VA-05, 5.2.3]
15. Statements, confirmations and document controls at VCB
VCB permits statements for a single VA or multiple VAs, selected over a user-specified from/to period, with or without a digital signature where supported. The document matrix allows PDF, XLSX and CSV for virtual account statements; a digital signature is available only on PDF. The BRD calls out the existing 10,000-record statement limitation and requires support for high-volume statements, but it does not prescribe whether the bank will use asynchronous generation, file splitting or a different approach. Those details belong to solution design once the business expectation and limits are agreed. 
A statement may need the remitter's information, receiving-bank context, beneficiary VA and Current Account details, transaction timestamp, reference, amount, currency and fee breakdown including VAT, as specified in the BRD's document matrix. That is richer evidence than a basic transaction list and explains why it is often used for ERP reconciliation or audit. The BRD flags that signatures may attract a fee and directs the reader to the Fees & Charges BRD for the actual rules. Nothing in the virtual account BRD determines the charging event, payer account, tax calculation, waiver or response handling.
Account information confirmation serves a different purpose. VCB permits one or more accounts to be included, but only Active accounts, and says the request is for the same day. The output format is PDF, with optional PDF digital signature. “Same day” still needs a precise interpretation: it may constrain the date for which existence is certified, when a request may be placed, or both. Without that decision, an apparently detailed processing rule could be wrong.
16. Other servicing features and notifications
A corporate user may designate up to ten favorite accounts and create, amend or delete nicknames up to thirty characters as set out in the in-scope section. The business-function descriptions refer to these limits as recommended, so a BA should confirm whether the limits are hard enforcement rules or interface guidance. Column arrangements are retained in the user's profile until changed or reset. These preferences should not alter the underlying account ownership, legal name or bank product data. 
The user can generate a QR-code image for a selected virtual account using its banking information, optionally enter an amount and payment description, then download or share the image within the app. What the QR code may actually contain depends on the approved payment scheme and payload standard, neither of which is specified in this BRD. The document describes the user-facing utility, not a universal QR payment acceptance rule. 
The BRD calls for real-time credit notifications to corporate users via Mobile, Email and Web. To interpret that correctly, the programme needs to know which credit event triggers the message, which users subscribe or are entitled, what happens if a transaction is reversed and how duplicate delivery is handled. These are natural questions for a notification service, not agreed answers in the present source. The document also requires logging of access and downloads with user identity and timestamp and retention for the applicable regulatory minimum period, while observing Vietnamese privacy obligations and VCB's data policy. 
One further item is a proposed global Smart-Search capability in the BRD's “Additional Feature Functionalities” section. It mentions fuzzy matching and searching transaction references or invoice identifiers across accounts, potentially finding the VA to which a receipt was attributed. This is materially different from the explicitly stated list search and filters. The section does not, on its own, establish whether Smart-Search has been accepted into the committed implementation scope. It should be kept visible for scope confirmation rather than automatically treated as another basic search rule.




17. Where the BRD needs a banking decision rather than a plausible assumption
Several points would materially affect the behaviour of the service if interpreted differently. The historical inquiry period is described as 3–5 years, without a single applicable figure. The balance fields coexist with a zero-opening convention and a product that does not hold independent funds. The list's nickname field is marked mandatory although nicknames are user-created. Account currency is described as the settlement-account currency in one field definition and VA currency in others. Entitlements can be configured at multiple hierarchy levels, but the precise aggregation and redirection rules for a user with partial visibility are not described. These are not matters to be “completed” by drawing on how another bank works. 
The permitted lifecycle statuses in the source are Active and Closed; closed VAs remain inquiry-visible for up to twelve months. That says nothing definitive about whether a transfer to a closed VA would be returned, held for investigation or posted elsewhere. Similarly, the inclusion of debit and credit values in transaction history does not establish that the inquiry channel can initiate VA debits. Account confirmation is restricted to Active accounts and same-day requests, yet the precise same-day basis is unspecified. Large statement volumes are required without an agreed technical generation strategy. These unresolved distinctions must remain visible until confirmed. 
18. How this knowledge should be used with the signed-off BRD
The broad domain explanation is there to teach the meaning of the product: why a corporate needs virtual accounts, which bank and corporate processes surround them, why a payment may be booked but not reconciled, and why virtual balances and physical funds cannot be conflated. The VCB section then identifies which parts of that domain are relevant to this particular inquiry and servicing implementation, using VCB's terminology and requirements.
In practical use, domain knowledge helps someone recognize an important question; it does not supply an unapproved answer. For example, the industry discussion of closed identifiers explains why historic mapping matters, but it is the VCB requirement that authorizes 12 months of visibility for Closed VAs. The industry discussion of subledger balances explains why a zero opening balance is plausible in reporting, but it does not determine VCB's exact running-balance formula. The discussion of incoming payment exceptions explains why payment repair matters, but it does not authorize building a payment investigations module under this inquiry BRD.
